AUGUST 17 • ENID'S WATER STORY • PART 4 OF 8

Enid Goes Looking for Water

The wells move farther from town, pipelines stretch across northwest Oklahoma, and Enid’s search for groundwater becomes a question of rights as well as engineering.

By the middle of the twentieth century, Enid had learned an uncomfortable truth: the city could not solve every water problem by drilling another well near town.

The names on Enid’s water map moved outward — Carrier, Drummond, Ames, Cleo Springs, Ringwood — and the pipelines grew longer.

A pipeline 19 miles long

By the time Bowles v. City of Enid reached the Oklahoma Supreme Court in 1952, Enid said it had already laid 19 miles of water main toward a new municipal well in Major County.

Ames and Okeene asserted interests of their own.

READ THE CASE

Bowles v. City of Enid (1952)

The Oklahoma Supreme Court upheld Enid’s authority to acquire land and groundwater rights for a municipal supply.

Five years later, Enid lost

City of Enid v. Crow showed the other side. Municipal need did not erase the rights of neighboring groundwater users. The court affirmed a judgment against Enid involving interference with a nearby supply.

READ THE CASE

City of Enid v. Crow (1957)

Read the decision ↗

This was not a story with heroes on one side and villains on the other. A growing city, farmers, towns and landowners were depending on connected water resources.

The map kept expanding

Enid continued developing distant wellfields. Some wells were shallow. Others were deeper. Some produced strongly; others aged, deteriorated or became less dependable. The municipal supply increasingly looked like a regional network rather than one city plant.

A future visual in this chapter will map that outward movement so readers can see the geography rather than simply read a list of wellfield names.

While Enid chased groundwater farther west, another possible source had been sitting on the map for decades: Canton Lake.
