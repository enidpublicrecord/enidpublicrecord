The 26-Foot Well

Enid decides to build a real municipal water system — and an early engineering report contains a number that sounds almost impossible.

1900

Seven years after Enid appeared on the prairie, the water question had changed. The question was no longer whether a growing city needed a municipal water system. It was how to build one.

$40,000 for waterworks

In 1900, Enid moved forward with a $40,000 waterworks program. In a young Oklahoma Territory city, that meant a major investment in pipes, pumps, mains, storage and wells.

It is in the engineering record of this period that we found one of the most remarkable details in Enid’s entire water story.

We thought it might be a typo

A contemporary national engineering publication described an Enid source well as 26 feet in diameter.

Twenty-six feet. Across.

That sounded extraordinary enough that we were reluctant to repeat it without checking whether wells of that scale actually belonged in Enid’s engineering history.

Then a later federal groundwater report documented another Enid municipal well approximately 20 feet in diameter and 47 feet deep, with brick curbing.

The records do not establish that these were the same well. They do establish something remarkable: Enid really did use enormous large-diameter municipal wells.

WHAT THE RECORD SUPPORTS
  • 1900: a $40,000 waterworks program.
  • Contemporary engineering report: an Enid well described as 26 feet in diameter.
  • Later federal record: a separate 20-foot-diameter, 47-foot-deep brick municipal well.

Enid Public Record does not identify any surviving structure or historical photograph as the 26-foot well without independent evidence.

The system went underground

Large wells were only part of the story. Federal records later described a central pump pit and underground collection galleries associated with Enid’s main Water Works. Those galleries contained rows of wells connected into a collection system and were expanded as demand grew.

That changes the mental picture. Early Enid’s water system was not simply a handful of narrow holes in the ground. Parts of it were engineered structures beneath the property.
