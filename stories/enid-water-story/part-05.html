AUGUST 18 • ENID'S WATER STORY • PART 5 OF 8

The Road Not Taken

Enid had municipal storage at Canton Lake — but legal access to water and the ability to deliver finished drinking water were never the same thing.

For decades, Enid had something remarkable on paper: municipal water-supply storage at Canton Lake. There was just one problem. The water was not in Enid.

The water was there. The pipeline wasn’t.

Having reservoir storage is not the same thing as having finished drinking water. Enid would need conveyance, pumping, treatment and enough downstream infrastructure to carry the flow.

Over the years, Canton returned to Enid’s planning discussions. The pieces did not always line up. Groundwater remained available, the capital investment was large, and the urgency changed with conditions.

Oklahoma City enters the story

Beginning in 1955, Oklahoma City used Canton storage through temporary federal arrangements because Enid had not developed it. In 1990, Enid and Oklahoma City reached an agreement, and Congress authorized reassignment of the storage subject to that agreement.

RECORDS REQUEST IN PROGRESS

The April 17, 1990 agreement

Enid Public Record is seeking the complete agreement and the financial records behind it. Until those documents arrive, this story will not state an unverified payment amount or assign a motive for Enid’s decision.

Canton came back into the plan

The City’s 2009 master plan later examined a Canton concept involving roughly 29 miles from Canton Lake to the Ames Plant, plus major capacity work in the existing Ames-to-Enid corridor.

Ames itself would need upgrades. The transmission system toward Enid would need additional capacity. Plant No. 1 would need to handle the increased flow and be capable of surface-water treatment.

So the Canton concept was never simply:

Lake → pipe → faucet.

SEE THE ENGINEERING

Canton → about 29 miles → Ames → about 15-mile transmission corridor → Enid → surface-water treatment.

A road not taken — more than once

Canton becomes more than a lost opportunity. It is a recurring road not taken, one that shows why legal access to water and the ability to deliver drinking water are two different things.

And Enid had another surface-water opportunity in its past.
