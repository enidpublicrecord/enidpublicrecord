AUGUST 20 • ENID'S WATER STORY • PART 7 OF 8

Enid Comes Back to Kaw

Long before the 70-mile pipeline, Enid already had a legal path to Kaw water. It did not build the system then. Decades later, the city came back.

There is a strange twist in Enid’s journey to Kaw Lake: Kaw was not a new idea. Enid had held a Kaw water permit before.

25,000 acre-feet a year

Enid’s old state permit was associated with 25,000 acre-feet per year — roughly 8.15 billion gallons annually, or about 22.3 million gallons per day averaged across a year.

Enid did not build the system needed to use it.

In 2001 the City authorized transfer of the permit to the Kaw Reservoir Authority. After a beneficial-use/non-use review, 14,159 acre-feet per year was transferred in 2002.

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

Canton’s federal reservoir-storage history and Kaw’s state water-permit history are not legally the same. This series does not flatten both events into a single claim that Enid simply “lost its water rights.”

The “old Enid” permit

By 2009, Enid’s engineers were looking at Kaw again. The master plan identified possible routes back to the source, including access through what it called the “old Enid” permit, a new state right, or another permit holder.

Modern Kaw required more than a state right. Enid also needed federal reservoir storage, approvals, property interests, treatment infrastructure and financing.

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers ultimately signed a contract with Enid in 2022 allocating 20,000 acre-feet of water-supply storage at Kaw.

PRIMARY FILE STILL BEING OBTAINED

Enid’s modern state Kaw water right

We are seeking the complete Oklahoma Water Resources Board administrative file so the final edition can document the modern state right, priority, conditions and acquisition history directly from the agency record.

Why go roughly 70 miles?

Groundwater was cheaper in the study. Canton was closer.

Kaw’s strategic advantage was different: it put part of Enid’s supply in the Arkansas River basin, exposed to a different hydrologic geography than the western Oklahoma sources on which Enid had historically depended.

During the severe 2011–2013 drought period examined by Enid’s engineers, Kaw’s lowest level was reported at only 1.11 feet below normal pool.

No reservoir is drought-proof. But that performance mattered.

WHAT “DIVERSIFICATION” ACTUALLY MEANS

Enid’s distant wellfields were geographically scattered, but they were still groundwater. Kaw added surface water from another watershed while keeping the wells available.

Enid had once possessed a path to Kaw water and never built the system to use it.

This time, Enid built the connection.
