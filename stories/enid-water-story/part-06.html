AUGUST 19 • ENID'S WATER STORY • PART 6 OF 8

The Wells Weren't Empty

The simple version says Enid was running out of groundwater. The engineering record says something more complicated — and more interesting.

There is an easy version of this story: Enid was running out of groundwater, so it had to go to Kaw. It is simple. It is memorable. And it is not quite what the record says.

7.3 million gallons per day

The 2014 supply study estimated sustainable production from Enid’s existing wellfields at about 7.3 MGD.

The wells were not empty. Additional groundwater remained a viable alternative — and its planning-level capital estimate was lower than Kaw’s.

But the same study described drought limitations, interference among wells, localized drawdown, aging infrastructure and the risks of continuing to depend overwhelmingly on one kind of source.

THE FACT THAT HAS TO STAY IN THE STORY

Groundwater remained an option.

A fair account of Kaw has to say this plainly. Kaw was not selected because engineers found literally no groundwater alternative.

Read the 2014 alternatives study ↗

Irrigation belongs here — carefully

Municipal wells do not pump in isolation. Agriculture and other users also depend on regional groundwater. That shared use matters, but the records we have reviewed do not justify assigning a specific percentage of Enid’s observed groundwater change to irrigation.

Likewise, this series does not attribute Enid’s groundwater decline to hydraulic fracturing without evidence establishing that connection.

Then the question changed

For generations, Enid had asked:

Where can we drill next?

The Kaw decision required a different question:

What if the next major source should not be another well?

That question becomes even more interesting once you know Enid had held a Kaw permit before.
