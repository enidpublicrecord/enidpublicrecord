PROJECT THUNDERBIRD • RECORD CHECK • ENID, OKLAHOMA

Project Thunderbird: What Enid Has Built — and What the Proposal Still Has to Prove

Enid already spent hundreds of millions securing a new water supply and millions more extending utilities to industrial land. Project Thunderbird now proposes to become a major user of some of those assets. The developers have put numbers behind several promises. The next test is which of them become engineering limits, contracts and enforceable public revenue.

On Aug. 21, Enid cut the ribbon on one of the largest public works projects in the city’s history: a new water system stretching roughly 70 miles from Kaw Lake to Enid.[1]

Less than three weeks later, Project Thunderbird became public.

The proposed development is ambitious: four data-center campuses, roughly 1,735 acres, a private power plant and a utility corridor described by project representatives as serving the campuses and supporting Vance Air Force Base. One proposed campus is the City-owned 66th Street Industrial Park, where Enid has already spent millions extending water and sewer infrastructure.[2][3]

At the Sept. 15 City Commission meeting, residents used public comment to raise concerns including water, nearby property, noise, jobs, infrastructure and accountability.[13]

Those concerns are part of the public record. They are not, by themselves, proof that the project will cause the harms residents fear. The same standard applies to project assurances: a promise is not proof that a problem has already been solved.

Rezoning is not construction approval.

The City says the matter currently under consideration is a zoning change. Rezoning would not authorize construction or operation of a data center.[4] A specific proposal would still face later review that may include site planning, technical studies, utility and infrastructure review, development agreements, permitting, public meetings and additional government action.[4]

There is not yet a selected data-center operator. Thunderbird’s materials say no operator has been chosen, and the City says the final developer and project details have not been finalized.[2][4]

THE WATER QUESTION

The water debate now has numbers.

Enid has just finished a water investment that took years to plan and hundreds of millions of dollars to build. The completed Kaw Lake Water Supply Program includes a raw-water intake, roughly 70 miles of pipeline and a 10.5-million-gallon-per-day treatment plant.[1][5]

At the Sept. 15 study session, City staff put the Kaw program’s total budget at $344,716,345.41. A City loan table showed $334,104,335.13 in original Kaw-related loans and $296,540,758.62 in principal still outstanding as of Aug. 1.[3][6]

Thunderbird’s developers say the project would become a major anchor customer on that system.[2]

At full buildout, Thunderbird says the four campuses and power plant would consume about 562 million gallons a year — an average of about 1.54 million gallons a day. The project estimates demand could reach 7.3 million gallons on the hottest summer day. Developers say they plan a relatively steady 2 million gallons per day raw-Kaw draw and roughly 31.8 million gallons of on-site storage to cover peaks.[2]

Project estimate These are Thunderbird’s current engineering estimates. Thunderbird says they are not permit limits and that detailed calculations are not finished.[2]

The City has not adopted those figures as an approved allocation. Its project page says any raw-water use remains subject to available supply, infrastructure capacity, contracts, permits and applicable approvals.[4]

So the question is no longer simply how much water Thunderbird says it needs. There is now a proposed answer. The harder question is whether Enid can reliably supply it while preserving the capacity the community will need in the future.

A 10.5-MGD treatment-plant rating is not a measure of spare raw-Kaw capacity for Thunderbird. The proposed data center would use raw water upstream of the drinking-water treatment process, and Enid has existing demand, regional customers and future-growth needs. That is why the formal capacity analysis, drought rules and water agreement matter.

Thunderbird says the computer campuses would use closed-loop cooling and the power plant would rely primarily on air cooling, with most consumptive loss during hot-weather turbine operation.[2] The design may reduce water use compared with some other cooling arrangements, but raw water remains part of the resource Enid spent hundreds of millions developing.

The next records that matter are the capacity study, the water contract, the drought provisions and the enforceable maximum withdrawal.

PUBLIC LAND + EXISTING INFRASTRUCTURE

The 66th Street connection predates Thunderbird.

Thunderbird identifies its proposed East Campus as the 66th Street Industrial Park, City-owned land the developers say they are interested in acquiring.[2]

At the Sept. 15 study session, City Engineer Jason Unruh presented the latest figures for the industrial park’s utility buildout. The City listed the waterline project at approximately $5.02 million, with about 21,616 feet of waterline. The sanitary-sewer project was listed at approximately $10.44 million, with about 19,052 feet of sewer line. Both were described as substantially complete.[3]

The City’s FY2025-26 budget had already described utility expansion at the 66th Street industrial park, establishing that the work was underway before Thunderbird was publicly announced.[7]

The record does not support saying Enid built those utilities for Thunderbird. It supports a different conclusion: Enid had already invested substantial public and grant-supported resources preparing the area for industrial development. Thunderbird is now one potential user of infrastructure already being built.

THE MONEY

The revenue claims are large. The final formulas are not public yet.

Thunderbird says the completed project could generate tens of millions of dollars a year at full buildout for schools, City and County services and infrastructure, with cumulative public revenue in the hundreds of millions over 25 years.[2]

Those are consequential claims. They are also project projections. Thunderbird labels its revenue projections as its own estimates, and the City says anticipated fiscal outcomes require further review as supporting documentation becomes available.[2][4]

EPR has not found a final public schedule showing what Enid Public Schools would receive each year, what other Garfield County districts would receive, what would go to the City or County, or how the totals change after incentives and tax exemptions.

Oklahoma offers incentives to qualifying data-center operations. State guidance says qualifying operations may receive sales-tax exemptions on equipment and machinery, with scope depending in part on the operator’s business classification. Certain qualifying computer/data-processing facilities may also receive a five-year ad valorem exemption.[8]

The five-year property-tax exemption does not simply mean local governments receive nothing; Oklahoma’s program provides reimbursement for qualifying exempt property taxes.[9] Still, the eventual operator, assessed property, eligibility, school-funding formula and any separate concession or community-benefit payments will affect the real public return.

Project projection Until those pieces are assembled in a public fiscal analysis and enforceable agreements, “hundreds of millions” is not booked public revenue.

What about Kaw debt?

Thunderbird says becoming an anchor customer would help Enid repay roughly $330 million in municipal debt.[2] The City’s current loan table adds necessary context: the Kaw loans shown by staff originally totaled about $334.1 million; after principal payments, about $296.5 million remained outstanding as of Aug. 1.[6]

So Thunderbird is not proposing to pay off a current $330 million principal balance. Its broader claim is that project-related water payments would help retire debt Enid is already carrying.

How much would go toward that debt each year? How quickly would it change the payoff schedule? Would it reduce the burden on the 1% Enid sales tax currently dedicated to Kaw-related costs? Those answers are not yet in a public executed agreement.[10]

Thunderbird also proposes a Water Restoration Fund surcharge and a Patriot Utility District supported through project concession fees.[2] Those mechanisms could become durable local revenue streams. Today, the final rate, allocation formula, payment schedule and enforcement terms are not public executed agreements.

THE RULES

Enid already created a second review gate.

Before Thunderbird was publicly announced, Enid had already changed its heavy-industrial zoning rules. On Aug. 18, the City Commission adopted Ordinance No. 2026-17, which specifically places data centers among uses requiring an Intensive Use Review and approval by the Mayor and Board of Commissioners.[11]

The timing is notable, but the record EPR reviewed does not establish that the ordinance was written specifically for Thunderbird. City commissioners had publicly discussed broader heavy-industrial amendments in July, covering multiple intensive uses and issues including water, power, noise, traffic and decommissioning.[12]

The ordinance requires an intensive-use applicant to identify its water source and provide estimated peak daily use and annual consumption. The review also addresses cooling technology, wastewater, electrical demand, traffic, emergency response, decommissioning and other infrastructure impacts.[11]

For qualifying intensive uses next to residential property, the ordinance includes a 500-foot structural setback and noise limits of 65 dBA during the day and 55 dBA at night, or the applicable pre-development level under the ordinance.[11]

An eventual Intensive Use Agreement may address drought curtailment, daily water-use limits, infrastructure responsibilities, public reporting and financial assurance for decommissioning.[11]

Adopted rule Project commitment What City law already requires, what Thunderbird is voluntarily promising and what ultimately appears in a signed agreement are not necessarily the same thing.

Thunderbird says financial obligations, buffers and community protections will be written into land-use entitlements so they survive future ownership changes.[2] If final documents do that, the public will be able to verify it. Until then, it remains a commitment about what the developers intend to put into those documents.

SEPT. 15 PUBLIC COMMENT

The meeting showed the questions residents want answered.

Residents raised concerns that included water, nearby property, noise, jobs, infrastructure and accountability. At least one speaker called for a moratorium.[13]

EPR’s review of the meeting recording found the comments were predominantly concerned or opposed, although not every speaker could be classified confidently from the recording.

Those statements should be reported for what they are: public concerns and claims made at a government meeting. They should not be converted into established facts without supporting evidence. The same rule applies to Thunderbird’s assurances.

The reporting standard here is not a verdict. It is a separation between claims, adopted rules, public records and the documents that do not exist yet.

WHAT COMES NEXT

Some of the most consequential documents are still ahead.

Project Thunderbird could become a major industrial customer for land, water and infrastructure Enid has spent years developing. Its developers have now supplied considerably more detail than they did at the announcement: proposed water volumes, a cooling strategy, a proposed utility structure, public-benefit commitments and a process for selecting a future operator.[2]

But there is no selected hyperscale operator. There is no executed Thunderbird water agreement. There is no final public fiscal-impact model showing exactly what each school system and local government would receive. There is no executed concession-fee schedule. And there is not yet a final Intensive Use Agreement turning project commitments into enforceable terms.

None of that means the commitments will fail. It means this is the stage at which they can still be measured.

What water is guaranteed to Enid first? What will Thunderbird pay for what it uses? How much new revenue will actually reach schools and local government after the tax structure is accounted for? What obligations survive a sale? And who pays if the project needs more infrastructure than expected?

The record does not support a final verdict on Project Thunderbird yet. It does support a much better set of questions.
