PUBLIC ASSET INVENTORY • ENID, OKLAHOMA

What Does Enid Own — and What Has It Built With It?

The short answer is not one number. Enid's public footprint includes hundreds of local property records, parks and public-safety facilities, an airport, industrial land, water and wastewater systems — and regional infrastructure that reaches far beyond Garfield County.

THE FIRST ANSWER

422 property records — but not 422 physical assets.

EPR preserved the complete 2025 Garfield County Treasurer owner-name universe for the City of Enid and four related public authorities. It contains 422 unique property IDs: 413 in the City-family result and nine under the Enid Municipal Authority, Enid Economic Development Authority, Vance Development Authority and Enid Public Transportation Authority.

The important distinction

A tax-roll property ID is not the same thing as a park, building, airport, well, pipeline or other physical asset. One facility can span multiple parcels. One tax account can represent an improvement rather than land. And major infrastructure can sit outside Garfield County entirely.

That distinction is why EPR did not publish a headline claiming “Enid owns 422 things.” The records support a more useful answer: 422 is a local real-property baseline. The actual public-asset system is larger, more varied and more interesting.

LOCAL REAL PROPERTY

Why the parcel count matters anyway.

The 422-record baseline is useful because it gives Enid residents a reproducible starting point for asking a simple question: where does public title show up in the county record?

The City-family portion contains 142 rural-format property IDs and 271 urban-format property IDs. The nine authority records are separate because public trusts can hold title in their own legal names even when the property serves a municipal purpose.

Current GIS does not line up perfectly with a certified tax roll from a prior year. Parcels split, merge or are reconfigured; owner fields can lag; and some assessment accounts are not land. The Atlas therefore follows a fail-closed rule: when the evidence did not establish a current geometry relationship, EPR did not invent one.

What this inventory does not claim: It is not a deed-by-deed proof of every acre Enid controls, it is not a count of every physical object the City owns, and it is not a single all-in municipal acreage total.

WHAT THE PUBLIC RECORD SHOWS

Enid has built an operating system, not just accumulated parcels.

The City's FY2025 audit provides a useful functional cross-check because it counts facilities by what they do, not by how many tax accounts happen to sit underneath them.

Parks & recreation

The audit reports 23 parks, plus a skate park, bike park, swimming pool, two splash pads, two soccer complexes, a disc golf course, a golf course and four fishing ponds.

That is why “more than 30 parks and recreation areas” on the City's broader Parks & Recreation page is not the same measure as the audit's 23-park statistic.

Public safety

The audit reports five fire stations, one police station, a police training center and a defense-tactics center.

The point is functional: multiple public-safety facilities can exist without one neat one-parcel-per-building relationship.

Woodring Airport

The audit reports two runways and 83 hangars at Woodring Regional Airport.

Some hangars appear in the tax system as improvement accounts rather than separate City land parcels, one reason raw tax-account counts can mislead.

Water & wastewater

The audit reports two water plants, three water towers and two sewer plants. Current City water records also describe roughly 140 producing wells, seven pumping stations and extensive collection and storage infrastructure.

The biggest example extends beyond the county line.

The completed Kaw Lake Water Supply Program includes a raw-water intake at Kaw Lake, roughly 70 miles of transmission pipeline, a 10.5-million-gallon-per-day advanced treatment plant and distribution-system improvements. A Garfield County parcel inventory can never fully describe a system that physically reaches across multiple counties.

That is the broader lesson of the asset inventory: the things residents use every day are often systems — roads, water, airport, public safety, recreation — whose legal ownership can be distributed across parcels, easements, facilities and public authorities.

LAND FOR FUTURE USE

Some of the most visible recent purchases are land Enid is still building toward.

Economic-development property east of Enid is the clearest example. City records and the 2024 annual report support a documented baseline of about 558 acres acquired through two major purchases: roughly 398 acres for the 66th Street Industrial Park and another roughly 160 acres west of it.

In 2025, official City records show two additional industrial-land agreements that became complementary 40-acre deeds in the west half of the northeast quarter of Section 12. The two closing payments totaled $469,332.34. EPR did not treat conflicting “560 acres” and “598 acres” statements in City records as proof of a mystery purchase; the deeds and payment records control.

Recent land buying also serves other public purposes. In 2025 Enid acquired two 20-acre West Rupe tracts tied to the multi-sports-complex area, a 62.44-acre runway-protection tract south of Vance Air Force Base, and a downtown parcel at Owen K. Garriott and Grand. Those four closings totaled $868,581.61 in confirmed disbursements, although one deed and payment posted after the FY2025 year-end.

The 62.44-acre Vance tract illustrates another evidence boundary: Enid formally offered the property to the Air Force, but the latest located completion records do not establish federal acceptance. The City remains the last documented surface owner in EPR's record.

WHOSE NAME IS ON THE TITLE?

“City property” can legally sit in more than one public entity.

Enid Municipal Authority, Enid Economic Development Authority, Vance Development Authority and Enid Public Transportation Authority are public trusts or component entities with their own legal identities. That means a municipal-purpose property can be titled to an authority instead of directly to “CITY OF ENID.”

For the main inventory, EPR groups those public-entity records together so readers can see the municipal asset footprint without pretending the legal entities are interchangeable. A separate future explainer can do the deeper work of showing how the trusts are governed, financed and connected to the City.

WHAT WE DIDN'T FORCE

There is still no responsible one-number answer for total assets or total acreage.

That is not a failure of the inventory. It is a feature of doing it carefully.

  • Parcels are not facilities. A park, airport or utility site can span several property records.
  • Assessment accounts are not always land. Some certified records are improvements on larger public sites.
  • Regional infrastructure crosses county boundaries. Kaw Lake intake and transmission assets sit outside the Garfield County property universe.
  • Easements and rights-of-way matter. A pipeline or road can depend on property rights that are not ordinary fee-title parcels.
  • Current GIS and a prior certified roll are different snapshots. EPR preserves those differences instead of silently substituting one for the other.

So the public-facing answer is deliberately layered: a certified local property baseline, a mapped Atlas, a functional facility inventory and clearly labeled regional infrastructure. Future records can make each layer better without rewriting leads into facts.

The result is not a perfect final list. It is a documented public starting point — one that can be checked, corrected and expanded as the record changes.
