Veterans Day 2026 · Enid, Oklahoma

From Wheat Fields to Wings: The Enid Stories Behind Veterans Day

An old newspaper, a family farm, a young pilot and the hometown hero whose name became Vance Air Force Base reveal how deeply military history runs through Enid, Oklahoma.

Enid Public Record | November 11, 2026

On September 18, 1942, an Enid newspaper gave its readers an unusual assignment for the next morning: search their homes for scrap metal, rubber and rags, and place the material along the curb before nine o'clock.

Old stoves, pipe, pots, pans, worn tires and broken tools were among the items listed. Army trucks from the local flying school were scheduled to make the rounds.

On the same front page, The Enid Events reported a wartime bond drive, a special showing of Mrs. Miniver at the Aztec Theatre for war-bond purchasers, and a $100,000 bond-sales goal for Enid and Garfield County that week. The goal was a target, not a reported final total. [1]

These were the details of everyday life in a community far from the battlefields of World War II. The scrap drives, public gatherings and bond campaigns were local ways of supporting a national war effort.

But just south of town, on land that had recently been a working farm, Enid's contribution was taking another form. Men were learning to fly.

The farm that became a flying school

Before the runways, barracks and aircraft, there was Ernest Baker's family farm on the south side of Enid.

In 1941, the Army was seeking places to train the large number of pilots it expected to need. Enid civic leaders worked to bring a flying school to the community, and Baker agreed to make his family's land available for the project. An Air Force historical account says he even allowed the deed to be transferred before he was paid, so work could move forward without delay. [2][3]

The city approved a $300,000 bond issue to help secure the site and meet the project's local infrastructure needs, including roads and utilities. That was not the total federal construction cost: the War Department separately announced a project cost of approximately $4 million. [3][4]

Construction began in July 1941. Then came December 7, when Japan attacked Pearl Harbor and the United States entered World War II.

One week later, on December 14, the first class of 63 aviation cadets arrived at Enid's new flying school. Training began two days later, even though construction was still underway. After their flying-school duties, cadets worked to clean the Oklahoma dirt and mud from their unfinished quarters. [4]

The installation was initially known as the Air Corps Basic Flying School at Enid. It became Enid Army Flying School in February 1942, and Enid Army Air Field in 1943. [5]

Over the years that followed, thousands of young aviators trained here. By the time the airfield was deactivated in January 1947, 9,895 pilots had earned their wings at the Enid installation, according to the Oklahoma Historical Society. [5]

That figure describes a military mission. The people behind it made the mission personal.

A son returns to his family's former land

Among the flying cadets who came to Enid was Ralph Baker, Ernest Baker's son.

Ralph had grown up on the family farm. A 2008 account by the 71st Flying Training Wing historian describes him returning as a cadet to find his childhood home transformed into a military installation, with aircraft flying over the land where wheat had once grown. [3]

An archival photograph shows Ralph standing beside a Vultee BT-13 Valiant, the type of training aircraft used at the school. The young man in that photograph was preparing for a war that would take him far from home. [2]

After earning his wings, Ralph served in the China-Burma-India Theater. Air Force histories credit him with more than 70 wartime missions, including flights along the dangerous Himalayan air route known as “The Hump.” One account says he once had to bail out over China. He received the Distinguished Flying Cross twice. [2][3]

Ralph survived the war and returned to Enid, where he lived with his family and remained part of the community. Decades later, the installation named its Baker Industrial Gate to honor the family's place in its history. [2][3]

There is a remarkable symmetry to the story: a father helped make land available for pilot training; his son learned to fly on that same ground; and after serving overseas, the son came home.

It is one family's experience, but it reminds us that wartime history was lived by people whose lives began in ordinary places.

The Enid native behind the name Vance

The name Vance is familiar to anyone who lives around Enid. Its history belongs to another family and another young aviator who did not make it home.

Leon Robert Vance Jr. was born in Enid in 1916. He later graduated from the United States Military Academy at West Point and served as an Army Air Forces officer during World War II. [6]

On June 5, 1944 — the day before Allied forces landed in Normandy — Vance led a bombing mission against German coastal defenses near Wimereaux, France. Anti-aircraft fire struck his aircraft, killing the pilot and wounding several crew members, including Vance. [6]

Despite a severe injury, he helped bring the badly damaged bomber back toward England and ordered the crew to parachute to safety. Believing a wounded crewman was still unable to jump, Vance chose to ditch the aircraft in the English Channel rather than leave the man aboard without a chance to survive. He was rescued from the water. [6]

For those actions, Vance was awarded the Medal of Honor posthumously. He had disappeared on July 26, 1944, when an aircraft carrying him home went missing over the North Atlantic. He was 27. [6]

On July 9, 1949, the airfield in his hometown received the name Vance Air Force Base in his honor. [5]

The name on the entrance sign is therefore more than a geographic label. It remembers a specific Enid resident and the decisions he made while serving others.

A community connection that endured

The end of World War II did not end Enid's role in military aviation. The base closed temporarily in 1947 and reopened in 1948 as pilot-training needs returned. Over the following decades, its aircraft, facilities and training methods changed, but aviation training remained central to its mission. [5]

Military families came to Enid for assignments that might last a short time or leave a lifelong connection. Civilian workers, instructors and support personnel became part of the community's relationship with the installation.

And Enid's military heritage is much broader than the airfield. Residents of Garfield County and northwest Oklahoma have served in every branch of the Armed Forces, in wartime and in peace. Some served in combat; others performed indispensable duties away from the battlefield. Their experiences differ, and none can be reduced to a single story.

At the Woodring Wall of Honor and Veterans Park, an 80-percent-scale replica of the Vietnam Veterans Memorial is a permanent reminder of that broader history. The memorial was dedicated in Enid on Veterans Day in 2013 and includes more than 990 names of Oklahomans who died or remain missing from the Vietnam War. [7]

It is a place to remember loss. Veterans Day also asks us to recognize the people who served and returned — neighbors, relatives, friends and coworkers whose service may not be visible in their everyday lives.

What November 11 asks us to remember

Veterans Day grew from the armistice that ended the fighting in World War I on November 11, 1918. Originally known as Armistice Day, the American observance was broadened in 1954 to honor veterans of all wars. [8]

This year, Enid's published Veterans Day program includes a World War II documentary presentation featuring 104-year-old B-17 veteran R.D. Lawrence and an evening Veterans Day Ceremony and Legacy Awards event. The city's schedule and event details are available through its official Celebrate Freedom page. [9]

The occasion is about gratitude, but it is also an invitation to listen. Some veterans have told their stories many times. Others have rarely spoken about their service. All deserve to be treated with dignity.

For Enid, that remembrance can begin with the places we know: a former farm south of town, an old newspaper in an Oklahoma archive, the memorial at Woodring and the aircraft that still cross the sky.

The history did not happen only somewhere else. A part of it happened here.

To every veteran in Enid, Garfield County, northwest Oklahoma and across the country: thank you for your service.

And to the generations who came before us, may we continue to remember not only what you did, but who you were.

— Enid Public Record

Look closely at this photograph.

A young man stands beside a military training aircraft, smiling for the camera. His name is Ralph Baker. And there's something remarkable about the ground beneath his feet. Not long before this photograph was taken, that ground was part of his family's farm.

In 1941, as America prepared for the possibility of war, Ralph's father, Ernest Baker, sold the family's farmland south of Enid to make way for a new military flying school. The city of Enid helped provide roads and utilities for the installation. Then came December 7, 1941. Pearl Harbor. One week later, the first 63 aviation cadets arrived in Enid. Military flight training began while construction was still underway. The country was at war. And this Oklahoma community had become part of the effort.

By September 1942, the local newspaper was asking Enid residents to place old metal, rubber, and other scrap materials along the curb. Army trucks would collect them. Local businesses helped promote war bonds. Even a special movie premiere at the Aztec Theatre became part of the wartime fundraising effort.

Meanwhile, at the flying school south of town, young men were learning skills they would soon need far from Oklahoma. Among them was Ralph Baker. After training on the land his family once farmed, he went overseas, flying more than 70 wartime missions in the China-Burma-India Theater. He survived the war and returned home to Enid. By the time the airfield closed temporarily in 1947, nearly 10,000 pilots had earned their wings there.

But there's another name woven into this history. Leon Robert Vance Junior. Born in Enid in 1916, Vance became a military aviator during World War Two. On June 5, 1944, his bomber was struck by enemy fire over France. The pilot was killed. Vance was severely wounded. Despite his injuries, he helped bring the crippled aircraft back toward England. He ordered his crew to parachute to safety. Believing a wounded crew member remained aboard, Vance chose to bring the aircraft down in the English Channel rather than abandon him. For his actions, he received the Medal of Honor. But Leon Vance never returned home. His aircraft disappeared over the North Atlantic during his journey back to the United States. In 1949, the airfield in his hometown was renamed Vance Air Force Base in his honor.

More than eight decades after that first class arrived, military aviation remains part of Enid's identity. But Enid's military history extends far beyond one airfield, one war, or one generation. It belongs to men and women from every branch of service. Those who served in combat. Those who served far from the battlefield. Those who returned home and built their lives here. And those whose names remain on memorials. Not every veteran's story involves a famous battle or a decoration for valor. Many are quieter than that. But each deserves recognition.

This Veterans Day, when we see the aircraft overhead, the flag flying downtown, or a veteran standing among us, we can remember that America's military history is also our local history. It happened here. On our farms. In our neighborhoods. And in the lives of people who called Enid home. From Enid Public Record— To every veteran, thank you for your service. And to every generation that came before us, may your stories never be forgotten.
