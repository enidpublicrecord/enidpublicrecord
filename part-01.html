Before There Was Enid, There Was Water

Before the pumps, reservoirs and pipelines, water was already shaping this place. Within Enid’s first year, the new city was debating how to build a municipal water system.

September 1893

Before there were streets, water towers, treatment plants or miles of pipe beneath Enid, there was a spring.

Long before anyone called this place Enid, travelers crossing what would become northwest Oklahoma knew there was water here. The Chisholm Trail passed through the area, and Government Springs became a place to stop.

Water was part of this place before the city was.

Then a city appeared

On September 16, 1893, the Cherokee Outlet opened. Almost overnight, tents, businesses and new claims transformed open prairie into a settlement.

Four days later, photographer Persis Gibbs stood south of the new town and made a photograph.

Four days. There was already an Enid to photograph.

A city can appear quickly. A water system cannot.

The newcomers could build tents, stores and houses. A growing city required something harder to improvise: a dependable water supply.

Government Springs belongs in that opening landscape. But the surviving record we have reviewed does not establish that Government Springs alone supplied the new city’s drinking water. That distinction matters. We will not turn a compelling opening into a claim the evidence cannot carry.

Nine months after the Run

By June 1894, Enid newspaper discussion was already describing municipal waterworks as a much-needed improvement. The argument was not simply whether waterworks would help. It was whether a city this young could afford them.

Think about the timing.

Enid was not yet a year old, and residents were already confronting a question that would follow the city for generations:

Where will the next gallon come from — and what are we willing to build to get it?

Enid did not remain a collection of tents. Buildings replaced temporary structures. Businesses needed dependable service. Homes needed water. Fire protection needed water.

A municipal system was coming.

And when Enid finally committed itself to building one, a national engineering report would describe a number so extraordinary we had to check it twice.

It was not 26 feet deep.

It was reported to be 26 feet across.
